Simple Tumble

Rolling cube puzzles for Unity that are generated, solved exactly and rated.

Simple Tumble builds a rolling cube puzzle from your settings, then proves it can be solved and works out its par, the fewest rolls it takes. The cube tips from tile to tile, and the puzzle is won with the right face down on the goal or pressed onto every target. It can add keys and doors, switches and gates, avoid and one-way tiles, multi-face tiles, turntables, crumbling tiles and portals, on square boards or shaped ones. Every puzzle is rated 1 to 5 stars and can be saved as a ScriptableObject, exported as a PNG or SVG picture, or played in the included 3D demo.

Version 1.0, by Living Failure

What it is

Simple Tumble is a rolling cube puzzle generator with a solver built in. A cube sits on a grid of tiles, and each roll tips it over one of its bottom edges onto the next tile north, east, south or west. Every roll changes which face is down, so getting the right face onto the right tile takes planning: the straight route usually arrives the wrong way up. You choose the board and what goes on it, and it builds a puzzle for you. Then it searches every position the puzzle can reach, so you know for certain that it can be solved and how many rolls the best solution takes.

The core doesn't care what the cube looks like. Tiles, faces and directions are plain numbers, so the same puzzle can be a die, a crate, a robot or anything else that tumbles. In the editor pictures and in the demo every face has its own shape as well as its own colour (a red dot, a blue square, a yellow triangle, a green diamond, a purple star and an orange hexagon), so colour-blind players can tell them apart.

Everything is deterministic. The same settings and the same seed always give the same puzzle, and every puzzle keeps the settings and seed it was made from. When the generator can't give you exactly what you asked for, it says so in the puzzle's notes instead of quietly handing you something else.

The rules

Reach the goal

Roll the cube onto the goal tile with the goal face down. Arriving with any other face down doesn't count, so the cube has to turn on the way.

Stamp the targets

Land on every target tile (1 to 8 of them) with the goal face down to stamp it. They can be stamped in any order, or in their numbered order as checkpoints.

Walls and holes block the cube the same way, and so does the edge of the board. A roll the rules don't allow simply doesn't happen, and the rules say why. Every gimmick follows the same idea: the cube is either allowed onto a tile or it isn't, and what it does there is fixed.

Features

Proven par

The solver searches every position the puzzle can reach, so the par is the true minimum and not an estimate. It also counts how many best solutions there are.

Difficulty in stars

A 1 to 5 star rating from the par and the turning the cube has to do beyond the plain walk. Ask for a star count and the generator picks the start to match.

Board size and shape

Boards from 2 to 24 tiles each way, as a full rectangle, a cross, ring, diamond, circle, heart, L, T or U, or any outline you type in, with walls and holes on up to 45% of it.

Keys, doors, switches and gates

Up to 4 keys, each opening its own door, and up to 4 switches that flip 1 to 3 gates each. Face locks make a key or a switch answer only one face.

Tricky tiles

Avoid tiles one face must never be down on, one-way tiles, multi-face tiles that need 2 or 3 faces pressed on them, turntables, crumbling tiles that break once the cube rolls off, and portal pairs.

Every gimmick matters

Each one is placed where it lengthens the puzzle, and the puzzle is checked so that none is left doing nothing. Turntables, crumbling tiles and portals are placed as tools the best solution needs.

No soft locks

Every puzzle reports whether the cube can reach a position it can't win from. NoSoftLocks, on by default, only accepts puzzles where that never happens.

Printable export

Save any puzzle as a PNG or SVG: the colour view, a black on white printable sheet that draws every face by its shape alone, or an answer key with the best solution drawn on as a line.

ScriptableObjects

Save single puzzles or ordered packs, with inspectors that preview, re-check and regenerate them.

Play rules included

TumbleRules tells your game what a roll does, using the same rules the solver counted with, so the game and the par always agree. It also gives hints from any position.

Editor tooling

One Tumble Generator window with 20 presets, an Auto button that fixes settings that fight each other, and generation on a background thread with a progress bar, Cancel and a time limit. Watch the best solution play in 2D or with a real 3D cube.

A 3D demo

Four game modes with endless puzzles and 44 ready-made levels, with sounds and effects, all built in code. It works in URP, HDRP and the Built-in pipeline.

How it works

1

Configure

Pick the board, the mode, the goal face and the gimmicks, in the Tumble Generator window or with a TumbleSettings object in code.

2

Lay out

Walls and holes are placed so the free tiles stay connected and keep the amount of turning room you asked for. Then a few goals (or sets of targets) are tried.

3

Solve and pick the start

One search back from the win gives the exact par from every position on the board. The start is picked from those, inside your par window and star band. Each gimmick then goes where it lengthens the puzzle most and the start is picked again, preferring one every gimmick matters to. A gimmick that still does nothing from that start is taken away, which never changes the par.

4

Rate and check

The finished puzzle is solved again from its start. It records the par, one best solution, how many best solutions exist, whether the cube can get stuck, and the stars. A puzzle that misses what you asked for is built again from the next seed.

5

Use it

Save it as an asset, export a picture of it, play it in the demo, or read the TumbleData in your own scripts.

Why "verified"

A rolling cube puzzle is easy to get wrong by hand. The cube can turn up the wrong way after a long walk, a key can sit where no one would ever bother fetching it, and a one-way tile can trap the cube for good. Simple Tumble proves the things you design around:

  • The par is real. It is the shortest solution that exists, so a 30-roll puzzle really takes 30 rolls.
  • Every position is searched. The generator gives the solver room for every position the board can hold, so its puzzles are always solved exactly and never cut short.
  • Getting stuck is measured. One-way tiles and switches can strand the cube. Every puzzle reports whether that can happen, and by default you only get puzzles where it can't.
  • Gimmicks earn their place. Taking any gimmick away would change the puzzle. A key you would never need, or an avoid tile off every best route, isn't left on the board.
  • Misses are reported. When a star count, a par window or a gimmick that doesn't fit can't be met, the puzzle's notes say what happened and what you got instead.

One honest limit: a few heavy settings take a long time to build, above all many targets in any order together with keys and switches, which can take a minute or more a board. The window has a time limit (60 seconds by default) and keeps the closest board that finished, with a note. Fewer targets, keys or switches, or a smaller board, build much faster.

Quick Start

From import to a puzzle you can play, in about five minutes.

1. Install

Import the package. Everything lives under Assets/SimpleTumble/, and nothing is added to your scenes until you ask for it.

The core (the generator, the solver, the play rules and the puzzle data) has no package dependencies. The demo uses the Input System and Unity UI packages. It reads the mouse, touch and keyboard through the Input System, so set Project Settings > Player > Active Input Handling to Input System Package or Both before you play it.

Render pipelines: URP is recommended, HDRP is supported and the Built-in pipeline still works. The demo builds its materials from the active pipeline's default shader and keeps that shader in your builds, and on HDRP it adds a small volume so it looks the same as on the others (turn off HdrpLook on the TumbleDemo component to use your own lighting instead). Simple Tumble was built and tested with Unity 6000.6.

2. Generate a puzzle

Open Window > Living Failure > Simple Tumble > Tumble Generator. You can also get there from Assets > Create > Simple Tumble > Tumble Generator.

1

Take a preset

Press Presets and pick Classic/Reach the goal: a 6 x 6 board where you roll the cube to the goal with the right face down. A preset replaces every setting with a ready-made recipe.

2

Generate

Press Generate. Puzzles are built and solved on a background thread, so the window stays responsive, and a long batch shows a progress bar with a Cancel button. Raise Batch count to make up to 50 at a time.

3

Try a few more

Classic/Checkpoints stamps 3 targets in their numbered order. Gimmicks/Keys and doors adds 2 keys, each opening its own door. Gimmicks/Portals adds a portal pair every best solution goes through, and Shapes/Heart cuts the board to a heart with 3 targets to stamp.

If you change settings by hand and they fight each other (more gimmicks than the cube's progress can track, face locks with nothing to lock, or no turning room at all), the window warns you, and Auto fixes them and tells you what it changed.

3. Read the analysis

The pane on the right shows the selected puzzle. From the top:

  • The verdict: the par in rolls (and "one best solution" when there is just one) and the difficulty out of 5, with a line that sums up the board and its seed. A warning shows when the cube can get stuck.
  • Board: a picture of the puzzle with a Roll slider above it that steps through the best solution, and Play to watch it roll by roll. Keys go, gates flip, faces press and tiles crumble as you drag it. Switch between 2D, where you can hover a tile to read it and zoom in, and 3D, a real cube on a 3D board that you can turn and zoom.
  • The cube, unfolded: beside the board, every face of the cube as it sits at that roll, what has to be down to win, and a plain-words list of what is on this board.
  • Export: save the puzzle as a PNG or SVG, as the colour view, a printable sheet or an answer key.
  • Best solution: every roll written out, with a Copy solution button.
  • Facts and Generation report: the numbers (tiles, turning spots, gimmicks, the plain walk, coverage and more) and every note the generator left.

Pressing Play in 3D is the quickest way to judge a puzzle. You can see the cube tip over each roll and how far out of its way it has to go to turn the right face down.

4. Save it

Press Save and choose:

  • Current saves the selected puzzle as a TumblePuzzleAsset.
  • Pack (all) saves every puzzle in the set as one ordered TumblePuzzlePack.
  • Pack (on target only) leaves out the fallbacks, the puzzles that missed something their settings asked for.

Both asset types have inspectors with the same analysis pane, plus Verify, which solves the puzzle again and refreshes its stored verdict, and Re-roll, which builds a new puzzle from the same settings with a fresh seed. A pack can also re-roll just its fallbacks, and Export all writes every puzzle and its answer key into a folder you pick.

5. Play the demo

Open Assets/SimpleTumble/Scenes/SimpleTumbleDemo.unity and press Play. The demo opens on a welcome screen. Press Let's play, pick a mode, then choose Endless for a new puzzle to your order or Level Pack for the ready-made levels.

The controls are also listed at the bottom of the screen while you play:

  • Arrow keys or WASD roll the cube, and so does a swipe. A tap beside the cube rolls it toward the tap. Each key rolls the way that looks closest to it on screen, so the controls stay right however the camera is turned.
  • U (or Backspace, or Ctrl+Z) undoes a roll, R restarts and H shows a hint.
  • F lets the solver play the rest of the puzzle from where you are, and F or R stops it.
  • [ and ] turn the camera, Home and End tilt it, the mouse wheel or + and - zoom, and right-drag turns and tilts. V resets the view.
  • M mutes the sound and Esc goes back to the menu.

The same actions are on the buttons at the bottom: Undo, Restart, Hint, Solve and Menu.

6. Use it in your game

Make a puzzle in code, check what the solver found, and read the rest straight off the TumbleData:

using SimpleTumble;

var settings = new TumbleSettings
{
    Width = 7, Height = 7,
    Keys = 1,                    // a key that opens a door on the way
    TargetDifficulty = 3,        // keep trying seeds until it rates 3 stars
};
TumbleData puzzle = TumbleGenerator.GenerateVerified(settings);
Debug.Log($"Par {puzzle.Par} rolls, {puzzle.Difficulty} stars, solvable: {puzzle.Solvable}");

To run your own game on it, keep a TumblePlay and ask TumbleRules what each roll does. These are the same rules the solver used, so your game always agrees with the par:

TumblePlay play = TumbleRules.NewGame(puzzle);
if (TumbleRules.TryRoll(puzzle, play, 1, out string reason))   // 0 north, 1 east, 2 south, 3 west
{
    if (TumbleRules.IsSolved(puzzle, play)) Debug.Log("Solved!");
}
else Debug.Log(reason);                                         // why not: a wall, a hole, a shut door...

if (TumbleRules.Hint(puzzle, play, out int dir))
    Debug.Log($"A best roll from here: {dir}. Face {Cube.FaceOn(play.Orientation, CubeSide.Bottom)} is down.");

The core numbers tiles from the bottom left, row by row (y * Width + x), and names faces 0 to 5, so a refused roll's reason reads like "the door at 23 needs key 0". The demo turns that into words for players, naming faces by their colour and shape.

Or let the demo's 3D view draw and play it for you. TumbleGame adds a TumbleBoardView to the same object and builds the whole board from code:

using SimpleTumble.Demo;

var game = new GameObject("Tumble").AddComponent<TumbleGame>();
game.Begin(puzzle);
game.View.FrameCamera(Camera.main);

A saved puzzle works the same way: give your script a TumblePuzzleAsset field and use its Puzzle, or a TumblePuzzlePack and its Get(index). See the API Reference for the rest.

Generating Puzzles

Every setting, what it does, and how the generator decides.

The Tumble Generator window

Open Window > Living Failure > Simple Tumble > Tumble Generator. The settings are on the left in five sections (Board, Goal, Gimmicks, Trim and Quality), with Auto, Presets and Reset above them and the seed, batch and Generate controls below. The puzzles you've made are on the right, one at a time with the analysis pane, and the arrows page through them.

Every puzzle the window makes goes through verified generation: if a board misses what you asked for, the generator builds another from the next seed, and if none of its tries succeed it gives you the closest one with a note saying why. Generation runs on a background thread with a progress bar that says which board it is on and what it is doing, and a Cancel button that stops it within a fraction of a second. Your settings and the unsaved puzzles survive a recompile.

Each table below gives the label in the window, the matching field on TumbleSettings for code, its range and its default. Values outside the range are clamped, so code can pass anything.

Every gimmick matters. After placing, each gimmick is tried taken away and the par compared. The start is picked again where the most of them change the puzzle, and one that still makes no difference is taken away (the par never changes when it is). A gimmick is only left in place when taking it away would let the cube get stuck, and then the note says so.

A one-goal puzzle uses about 17 tiles however big the board is, so on a big board most of it is scenery. Trim cuts the tiles far from the solution away and crops the board, so a big board becomes a compact shape round its puzzle.

How the stars work

Every proven puzzle gets a 1 to 5 star difficulty, or 0 when it isn't proven solvable. The score is the par plus 2 for every roll spent turning beyond the plain walk, where the plain walk is the shortest route to the goal with any face down (or the shortest tour over the targets). Walking is easy, and the extra rolls you spend getting the right face down are the part you have to plan, so they count three times.

Auto and presets

Auto fixes settings that fight each other and tells you what it changed. The window also shows what Auto would change, live, as a warning under the settings. In code it is TumblePresets.Tune. It:

  • cuts crumbling tiles, then multi-face tiles, then switches, then keys until the cube's progress fits in 10 bits;
  • turns Face locks off when there are no keys or switches to lock;
  • raises Turning room to 0.02 when it is lower, since with none the cube may have nowhere to turn;
  • raises a tight trim's Margin to 1 when it is 0, since that board gives the answer away;
  • sets the outline back to Full when custom rows don't make a board;
  • raises Target stars to what the keys and switches can go down to: with all of them in, 2 never rated below 3 stars when reaching the goal, and 3 or more never below 5 (or 4 when stamping targets), measured;
  • raises Target stars to 3 with a multi-face tile, which rarely rates lower.

Seeds, batches and saving

The same settings and the same seed always give the same puzzle. In the window, Random uses a fresh seed for every Generate and shows the one it used. Turn Random off to type a seed and keep it, and Copy copies it. Each puzzle in a batch gets its own seed, the batch's seed followed by a dash and the puzzle's number (-0, -1 and so on), and every puzzle stores its own seed, which the analysis pane can copy.

Verified generation tries the seed itself first, then the same seed with #1, #2 and so on added, until a puzzle meets the request. A puzzle's stored seed is the one that worked, such as level-7-5#1, so with the same settings it always rebuilds that exact puzzle.

Batch count makes 1 to 50 puzzles per click and adds them to the set. Clear on generate empties the set first, so each click replaces the batch. Time limit (0 to 600 seconds, 60 by default, 0 for none) is how long one puzzle may take: past it, generation keeps the closest board that finished, with a note, and if no board finished, that puzzle is listed as not made and the batch carries on. The limit is shared with the inspectors' Re-roll.

Only on target hides the fallbacks, and the cross removes a puzzle from the set. Save then writes Current as a TumblePuzzleAsset, or Pack (all) or Pack (on target only) as a TumblePuzzlePack, using the name in Save name as the suggestion.

Limits and notes

The generator never quietly gives you less than you asked for. Whenever something didn't go to plan, the puzzle's Notes say what happened, and the analysis pane shows every one. Each note starts with what it is about:

  • Target difficulty and Difficulty: no puzzle in the attempts had the stars you asked for. You got the closest, with a hint which way to change the settings. A tight trim can also move a puzzle off its star target, and the note says so.
  • Verified: no puzzle in the attempts was solvable inside your par window, coverage and soft lock rule, or none had room for every gimmick you asked for.
  • Keys, Switches, Avoid, One-ways, Multi-face tiles, Turntables, Crumbling tiles, Portals and Targets: the board only had room for some of them, and the note says how many and why.
  • Gimmicks: some gimmick made no difference from the best start and was taken away, or stays because taking it away would let the cube get stuck.
  • Par: the board's longest puzzle was shorter than your minimum, or no start fitted under your maximum.
  • Trim: the margin was widened, or the board kept whole, so the cube can't get stuck, or a key or switch off the kept solution went with the cut tiles.
  • Shape, Obstacles and Start: the outline or custom rows left too few tiles, there wasn't room for every obstacle, or no position on the board can win at all.
  • Time limit: generation stopped at the time limit, and this is the closest of the boards that finished.
  • Solver: the state budget ran out before the search finished, which only happens when your own code calls the solver with a budget smaller than the board (the generator, Verify and hints never do), or there are more than 1,000,000,000 best solutions and the count stops there.

Use TumbleAudit.MeetsRequest(puzzle) to check whether a puzzle got everything its settings asked for, and TumbleAudit.Missing(puzzle) to list what it missed. A pack's inspector can re-roll every puzzle in the pack that missed something (Re-roll > Fallbacks).

Generating from code

Everything the window does is available from code through TumbleGenerator. Your settings object is copied and clamped first, so it is never changed.

using SimpleTumble;

var s = new TumbleSettings
{
    Width = 8, Height = 8,
    Mode = TumbleMode.StampAll,
    Targets = 3,
    Ordered = true,               // numbered checkpoints, stamped in order
    TargetDifficulty = 4,
    Seed = "level-7",             // empty = a fresh random seed
};

TumbleData one = TumbleGenerator.Generate(s);            // one puzzle from this exact seed, solved and rated
TumbleData good = TumbleGenerator.GenerateVerified(s);   // tries seeds until the puzzle meets the request
List<TumbleData> pack = TumbleGenerator.GenerateBatch(s, 10);   // seeds level-7-0 to level-7-9, each verified

Generate, GenerateVerified and GenerateBatch each have an Async version that runs on a background thread and takes a CancellationToken. In Unity, await carries on on the main thread afterwards. GenerateBatchAsync also reports progress:

var progress = new Progress<int>(n => Debug.Log($"{n} of 10 done"));
List<TumbleData> puzzles = await TumbleGenerator.GenerateBatchAsync(s, 10, progress, cancel.Token);

For a time limit and a running report of what a board is doing, use GenerateVerified(settings, cancel, progress, timeLimitSeconds), which the window uses. See the API Reference.

How long it takes (measured on a desktop, 5 builds of each preset; a phone is slower): plain boards take 2 to 30 milliseconds, boards with keys, switches, turntables or crumbling tiles 20 to 300, portals about 80, and the Everything preset 0.3 to 0.7 seconds. The slow case is many targets in any order together with keys and switches, which can take from several seconds to over a minute a board. In a game, generate with the Async versions (the demo does) or ahead of time in the editor as a pack.

A puzzle by hand

Fill in the board and the puzzle, check it with TumbleRules.Validate, then let the solver write the verdict. This 5 x 4 board puts the key in the top left corner and a door on both ways into the goal, so the cube has to fetch the key first. The comments show what it prints.

using System.Collections.Generic;
using System.Linq;
using SimpleTumble;
using UnityEngine;

var m = new TumbleData
{
    Width = 5, Height = 4,
    Mode = TumbleMode.ReachGoal,
    Start = 0,                          // cell (0, 0), bottom left
    StartOrientation = Cube.Standard,   // face 0 on top, face 5 down
    Goal = 4,                           // cell (4, 0), bottom right
    GoalFace = 0,                       // win with face 0 down
    Walls = new List<int> { 7, 12 },
    Keys = new List<int> { 15 },        // key 0 on cell (0, 3), top left
    Doors = new List<DoorTile> { new DoorTile(3, 0), new DoorTile(9, 0) },   // both ways into the goal
};

if (!TumbleRules.Validate(m, out string error)) Debug.LogError(error);
TumbleSolver.Verify(m, TumbleSolver.StatesOnBoard(m));   // a budget this big never cuts the search
Debug.Log(TumbleAudit.Short(m));                          // 5 x 4, reach the goal, par 10, 1 star, unique
Debug.Log(string.Join(" ", m.Moves.Select(d => "NESW"[d])));   // N N N S E S S E E E

Without the key and its doors the same board takes 8 rolls, so the key really is part of the puzzle. Rolling east from the start runs into the shut door on the third roll, and TryRoll refuses it with "the door at 3 needs key 0".

In Endless, Next makes a new puzzle to the same order, and in a pack it goes to the next level, then back to the pack after the last one. Menu or Esc returns to the mode cards at any time.

Playing

The controls are listed at the bottom of the screen while you play, and in the Quick Start. A few details:

  • Rolling: the arrow keys, WASD and swipes roll the cube the way that looks closest on screen, so the controls stay right however the camera is turned. A tap beside the cube rolls it toward the tap. A key pressed mid-roll waits for the roll to land.
  • The gimmicks react: a key spins up and away and its doors sink open, switch buttons go down and gates sink or rise, ticks pop up on stamped targets and pressed faces, a crumbling tile drops once the cube rolls off it, the cube shrinks into a portal and grows out of its twin, and a turntable turns with the cube on it.
  • A refused roll rocks the cube toward that way, and the card says why in plain words: "A wall is in the way.", "Door 1 is locked: take key 1 first.", "Gate 2 is shut: press switch 2 to open it." or "The green diamond mustn't touch down on that tile." Faces are named by their colour and shape.
  • Undo plays the last roll backwards, with every gimmick back as it stood. Restart pops the cube back in on its start.
  • Hint floats a blue arrow over the cube the way a best solution rolls first. When there is no way to win from where you are, the card says "There's no way on from here: undo or restart."
  • Solve (or F) plays the rest by itself, one best roll at a time, starting again from the beginning if you have rolled into a dead end. The button says Stop meanwhile, and F or R stops it.
  • The view: the camera frames the board in the space the HUD leaves free, in landscape or portrait, and again whenever the window changes shape. Turn, tilt and zoom it with the keys or the mouse, and when zoomed in it follows the cube.
  • The rating on the win panel is 3 pips on par, 2 a little over (up to 2 rolls, or a quarter of the par on longer puzzles) and 1 beyond that.

Sounds and effects

TumbleBeeper makes every sound in code when the demo starts: a wooden tok as the cube lands (three variants, so a run of rolls doesn't sound robotic), a dull bump for a refused roll, a sparkle for a key, a sliding rumble for a door or gate, a click for a switch, a stamp and a chime for a target, a ping for a face pressed, a whirr for a turntable, a whoosh for a portal, a crackle for a crumbling tile, a hint, a rewind for undo and restart, a win arpeggio and a button blip. M mutes it. There is no music.

TumbleEffects adds small effects made from a pool of tiny cubes: dust where the cube lands or bumps, sparkles over a key, a stamped target or a pressed face, dust round a door or gate as it sinks, a swirl at a portal, chunks from a crumbling tile, and confetti over the whole board for the win. Nothing is created or destroyed while you play, and because the effects use the demo's ordinary materials, they look the same in every render pipeline.

Render pipelines

The demo works in URP (recommended), HDRP and the Built-in pipeline without any changes. Its materials use the active pipeline's default lit shader, and a build step keeps that shader in your builds, so the demo doesn't draw pink on a phone. The faces, numbers and marks are baked into lit textures, so nothing needs an unlit or text shader.

HDRP lights in physical units, sets the exposure on its own and takes its ambient light from a sky, so the demo would come out dark and tinted. With HdrpLook on, DemoHdrp adds a global volume with a fixed exposure, no tone mapping or bloom, and a gradient sky in the demo's colours, clears the camera to the demo's background colour, and sets the sun in lux. That code only compiles when the HDRP package is installed.

The shipped scene was checked in all three pipelines: every material on the pipeline's lit shader, nothing pink, and the pictures within a few shades of each other.

Using the pieces yourself

The demo components work on their own too. The smallest setup is one TumbleGame, which adds its TumbleBoardView automatically:

using SimpleTumble;
using SimpleTumble.Demo;

var game = new GameObject("Tumble").AddComponent<TumbleGame>();
game.Begin(puzzle);                                    // build the board and start a game
game.View.FrameCamera(Camera.main, pitch: 50f);        // the camera is yours: frame the board once
game.Message += text => Debug.Log(text);               // a refused roll, in the player's words
game.Won += g => Debug.Log($"Solved in {g.Moves} rolls, par {g.Par}");
game.Roll(Cube.East);                                  // roll from your own code (keys, swipes and taps work too)
  • Input: TumbleGame reads the keys, swipes and taps itself while it is enabled. To drive it yourself, turn off KeyboardInput, PointerInput or both. Your code can roll either way, with Roll(dir), Push(screenDirection) and Tap(screenPoint), and call Undo(), Restart(), ShowHint() and StartAutoSolve(). Set PointerBlocked so a press over your own UI doesn't roll the cube.
  • The camera is yours unless you turn on CameraControls, which lets the game frame the board and gives players the turn, tilt and zoom controls. FrameAreas keeps the board in the parts of the screen your UI leaves free.
  • Events: Moved after every change of position, Won once when it is solved, Message for things a player should read, Cue for every moment worth a sound, and Moment for the same moments with a position and colour for effects.
  • A HUD: add a TumbleHud and set its Game, or read Moves, Par, Solved, HintsUsed and CanUndo into your own UI.
  • The look: the colours and materials are in DemoLook.cs, the meshes in TumbleMeshes.cs and TumbleBoardView.cs, and RollTime sets how long a roll takes (0.24 seconds by default).
  • Without the 3D view: keep a TumblePlay and use TumbleRules directly, as in the Quick Start.

Troubleshooting

What the notes mean, why a request can miss, and how long things take.

Reading the notes

Whenever the generator can't give you exactly what you asked for, it writes a note on the puzzle instead of quietly giving you less. The analysis pane shows every note, and so does TumbleData.Notes from code. Each one starts with what it is about, and the Generating page lists them all. TumbleAudit.MeetsRequest(puzzle) tells you whether a puzzle got everything its settings asked for, TumbleAudit.Missing(puzzle) lists what it missed, and a pack's Re-roll > Fallbacks replaces the ones that missed.

"Target difficulty: 5 stars not found in 40 tries; closest was 4."

The stars come from the par and from the rolls the cube spends turning beyond the plain walk, so they follow the board: a bigger board makes longer puzzles, and fewer places to turn on the spot make more turning work. The note ends with what to try: a bigger or smaller board, more or fewer obstacles, or less turning room. Also:

  • raise Max attempts under Advanced, so more seeds are tried;
  • for fewer stars, use fewer gimmicks. Gimmicks add rolls, so puzzles with them rate higher, and the note says so when that is why it missed. Measured, a ReachGoal puzzle with two keys or switches rarely rates below 3 stars and one with three or more rarely below 5 (below 4 when stamping targets), and a multi-face tile rarely goes below 3. Auto raises the target to those floors and says why.

A Tight trim re-rates the stars after cutting the board, so it can move a puzzle off its target. The puzzle then carries a "Difficulty:" note saying so.

"Verified: no puzzle in 40 tries ..."

Two notes start this way.

  • "... was solvable with a par of 30 or more rolls ...": no board was sound, meaning solvable, inside the par window and the coverage, and with no way to get stuck when No soft locks is on. The note gives the closest puzzle's par and coverage. Widen the par window, lower the minimum coverage, use a bigger board, or fewer obstacles.
  • "... had room for every gimmick asked for ...": every board was short of at least one gimmick, and the notes above it say which. A bigger board or fewer gimmicks fits.

Auto fixes the settings known to fight each other, and the window shows what it would change before you press it.

Fewer gimmicks than asked

Every gimmick is placed where it makes the puzzle longer, and the start is then picked so that every one of them matters. That is why a puzzle sometimes has fewer than you asked for:

  • "Keys:", "Switches:", "Portals:" and the like say the board only had room for some of them, and why: no tile where one would lengthen the puzzle, or nowhere left to put it. Bigger boards, fewer obstacles or fewer of that gimmick help. Verified generation tries more seeds first, and the window's Only on target keeps just the puzzles that got everything.
  • "Gimmicks:" says a gimmick made no difference from the best start and was taken away, which never changes the par. It can also say a gimmick was left on because taking it away would let the cube get stuck.

Turntables, crumbling tiles and portals are placed as tools the best solution has to use, so they need room for a route through them, and a crowded board may not have one.

Too many targets, keys and switches

Targets, keys, switches, the faces of multi-face tiles and crumbling tiles each double the positions the solver has to weigh, so together they are limited to 10. The window counts them for you, and Auto cuts the last ones placed (crumbling tiles first, then multi-face faces, switches and keys) until they fit. Avoid tiles, one-way tiles, turntables and portals don't count toward the limit.

"Can get stuck"

A plain board can't trap the cube, because every roll can be rolled back. One-way tiles, switches, turntables and crumbling tiles can, because what they do can't be undone by rolling back. The solver checks every position and reports CanGetStuck when a player could reach one that can never be won.

  • No soft locks is on by default, and then only puzzles where it can't happen are kept.
  • Turn it off for harder puzzles where a wrong move can cost the game, and give players Undo and Restart, as the demo does. Its hint also says when there is no way on from where the player is.

Generation feels slow

Measured on a desktop PC outside Unity, 5 builds of each preset (your machine and Unity's runtime will differ, and a phone is slower):

PuzzleTime for one verified puzzle
Every other preset: plain, shaped and trimmed boards, targets, keys, switches, avoid, one-way and multi-face tiles, turntables2 to 46 ms
Portals73 to 95 ms
Crumbling tiles39 to 291 ms
The Everything preset0.3 to 0.7 s

The slow case is many targets in any order together with keys and switches, which can take from several seconds to over a minute a board. Fewer targets, keys or switches, or a smaller board, build much faster.

  • In the window, generation runs on a background thread with a progress bar that says what the board is doing, and Cancel stops it within a fraction of a second. The Time limit (60 seconds by default) keeps the closest board that finished, with a "Time limit:" note.
  • In a game, use the Async methods, as the demo does for Endless, or make levels ahead of time in the editor and ship them as a pack.
  • Hints solve from where the player is. In the demo's checks, on 20 puzzles from 10 presets, the slowest hint took 4 ms.
  • On WebGL there are no threads, so the demo makes Endless puzzles on the main thread there, and the game pauses while it does.

"Solver: the search stopped at ... states"

The generator, the inspectors' Verify and hints always give the solver room for every position the board can hold, so you never see this on their puzzles. It only happens when your own code calls TumbleSolver with a budget smaller than the board. Pass TumbleSolver.StatesOnBoard(puzzle) as the budget and the search is never cut.

"Solver: more than 1,000,000,000 best solutions; the count stops there." only means the count is capped. It happens on big open boards, where many best solutions differ only in the order of a few rolls.

The demo ignores taps or keys

The demo uses the Input System package. Set Project Settings > Player > Active Input Handling to Input System Package or Both. The hub makes an EventSystem with the Input System's UI module when the scene has none. If your scene already has an EventSystem, its input module has to match your input handling. Presses on the HUD's card and buttons never roll the cube.

Pink or dark in a pipeline

  • Pink in a build: the demo makes its materials in code, and a build strips shaders that only code uses. Before every build, TumbleLookKeeper writes Runtime/Demo/Resources/SimpleTumbleLook.mat with the active pipeline's lit shader, so the build keeps it. If you see pink, check that file exists after a build (deleting it is fine: the next build writes it again).
  • Dark or tinted in HDRP: out of the box HDRP uses physical light units, automatic exposure and sky lighting. TumbleDemo.HdrpLook (on by default) adds a global volume at priority 10 with a fixed exposure and a gradient sky, and sets the sun in lux, so the demo looks the same as on the other pipelines. Turn it off to let your own volumes and lights take over.

Resetting the demo's progress

The demo saves each pack level's best rolls in PlayerPrefs, under SimpleTumble.Demo.Best.<pack name>.<level>. Call TumblePackProgress.Clear(packName, count) to forget a pack, or delete those keys. A pack's progress is filed under its pack name, so renaming a pack starts it fresh.

Seeds and versions

The same settings and seed always give the same puzzle within a version of Simple Tumble. A future version that improves the generator may change what a seed produces. Saved assets store the puzzle itself, not just its seed, so your saved puzzles and packs never change. Verified generation stores the seed that actually worked, such as level-7-5#1, so copying a puzzle's own seed always rebuilds that exact puzzle.

Console messages

  • "[Simple Tumble] Documentation page not found": the documentation menu looks for these pages in a folder whose path contains SimpleTumble and Documentation. Keep the Documentation folder inside the Simple Tumble folder.
  • "[Simple Tumble] Generation failed": something went wrong on the window's generation thread. Please send the message with your settings and seed.
  • "[Simple Tumble] Kept ... in the build for the demo": the build step wrote the look material, which is expected after you switch render pipelines.
  • "[Simple Tumble] No lit shader found for the active render pipeline": the pipeline asset has no default material, so the demo may draw pink in a build. Give the pipeline asset a default material.
  • "[Simple Tumble] Exported 12 file(s) from ...": a pack's Export all finished.
  • "Simple Tumble hub: no picture for ...": a mode card's picture couldn't be drawn. The card still works without it.

About Simple Tumble

Simple Tumble is a rolling cube puzzle generator for Unity, made by Living Failure. It builds puzzles where a cube tips from tile to tile and has to land the right face down on the goal, or press it onto every target, on square or shaped boards with keys and doors, switches and gates, avoid and one-way tiles, multi-face tiles, turntables, crumbling tiles and portals. Then it solves each puzzle exactly, stamps it with its proven par and a 1 to 5 star difficulty, and stores it as a ScriptableObject or exports it as a picture. A complete 3D demo, built entirely in code, is included.

Version 1.0, made and tested with Unity 6000.6 in URP, HDRP and the Built-in pipeline.

Design principles

  • Solve exactly. The par, the number of best solutions and whether the cube can get stuck come from searching every position a puzzle can reach, not from an estimate.
  • Measure, then promise. The star bands, the presets, Auto's fixes and the demo's difficulty recipes all come from measuring real puzzles, hundreds at a time, and the docs say so where it matters.
  • Be honest. When a request can't be met, the puzzle's notes say so. Nothing is quietly downgraded, and the store page claims nothing the solver can't back up.
  • Every gimmick matters. A key, a switch or a tile that changes nothing about the puzzle isn't left on the board.
  • One set of rules. The generator, the solver and the play rules your game uses all roll the cube the same way, so your game and the par always agree.
  • Not by colour alone. Every face has a shape as well as a colour, and every gimmick its own shape, in the pictures and in the demo, so colour-blind players can play every puzzle.
  • No bloat. The core has no package dependencies, and the demo and the editor tools live in their own assemblies. The demo needs no prefabs, models, textures or audio files.
